# Revenue Streams

## Standard VC Fund Revenue Model

PhyCap operates as a venture capital fund with the standard two-component revenue structure. Understanding this model is important both for internal planning and for physician LP education, since many physician investors encounter VC fund economics for the first time with PhyCap.

---

## Revenue Component 1: Management Fee

**What it is:** An annual fee charged to the fund's committed capital (and later, invested capital) to cover operational expenses. This is the fund's operating revenue during the investment period.

**Standard range:** 1.5% to 2.5% of committed capital annually during the investment period. After the investment period, the fee typically steps down and may be calculated on invested capital rather than committed capital.

**For a $10M fund:** At a 2% management fee, annual operating revenue is approximately $200K during the investment period. This covers GP compensation, fund administration, legal, compliance, travel, and marketing/brand activities.

**Key consideration for Fund I:** Management fee revenue at a $10M fund size is modest. This constrains operational spending and means the team must be efficient with brand, content, and event investments. It also means GPs are likely earning below-market compensation during Fund I, which is typical for emerging managers and reflects a bet on carried interest.

---

## Revenue Component 2: Carried Interest (Carry)

**What it is:** The fund's share of investment profits, paid after LPs have received their capital back plus a preferred return (the "hurdle rate").

**Standard structure:**
- Hurdle rate: Typically 8% annual preferred return to LPs
- Carry percentage: Typically 20% of profits above the hurdle
- Timing: Carry is realized only when portfolio companies are exited (acquisition, IPO, or secondary sale) and profits exceed the hurdle

**For a $10M fund:** If Fund I returns 3x ($30M), the gross profit is $20M. After the 8% preferred return and GP catch-up, the carry pool is approximately $4M (20% of qualifying profits), distributed across GPs according to carry allocation agreements.

**Key consideration for Fund I:** Carry is the primary economic incentive for GPs but is realized 5-10 years into the fund's life. This long timeline is important context for GP commitment and for LP understanding of alignment.

## Physician LP Economics: Their Perspective

### Who the Physician LP Is

PhyCap's LP base includes practicing physicians who are, in many cases, first-time VC fund investors. They are high-income professionals (typically $250K-$600K+ annually depending on specialty and practice setting) who have limited experience with illiquid alternative investments. Their investment decisions are shaped by factors that institutional LPs do not face.

### Investment Parameters

**Minimum investment:** Fund-specific, but emerging healthcare VC funds targeting physician LPs typically set minimums in the $25K-$100K range to balance accessibility with fund administration efficiency. The specific minimum should be documented in fund offering materials.

**Capital calls:** LP commitments are not funded all at once. Capital is called over the investment period (typically 3-5 years) as the fund deploys capital. A $50K commitment might be called in 4-6 installments over 3 years. This is important for physicians to understand because it means the full commitment amount does not leave their bank account on day one.

**Hold period:** Venture capital funds are typically structured as 10-year vehicles with optional 1-2 year extensions. Physician LPs must understand that their capital is illiquid for the duration. There is no early withdrawal mechanism comparable to selling public stocks.

**Expected returns:** Seed-stage healthcare VC funds targeting 3x-5x net returns over the fund life are performing well. Individual investments within the portfolio may return 0x (total loss) or 10x+ (exceptional outcome). The portfolio approach averages these outcomes. Physician LPs should understand that:
- Venture capital returns follow a power law distribution
- Early fund life often shows paper losses (the "J-curve")
- Meaningful returns are typically not realized until years 5-10

### Physician-Specific Considerations

**Student debt context:** Many physicians carry $200K-$400K+ in student loan debt. VC fund commitments compete with debt repayment for discretionary capital. PhyCap's LP education should address how physicians at different career stages (resident, early-career attending, mid-career, late-career) think about alternative investment allocation alongside debt obligations.

**Tax considerations:** VC fund investments have specific tax implications (K-1 reporting, capital gains treatment, carried interest taxation) that differ from the W-2 income and real estate investment structures most physicians are familiar with. PhyCap should recommend that physician LPs consult their own tax advisors, not provide tax advice directly.

**Familiarity gap:** Most physicians have no experience with:
- Capital call mechanics
- LP Advisory Committee (LPAC) governance
- Fund reporting (quarterly/annual letters, capital account statements)
- Vintage year benchmarking
- J-curve dynamics

This familiarity gap is not a weakness of the physician LP base. It is a LP education opportunity that, when handled well, builds trust and loyalty. Physicians who feel respected and educated throughout the process become long-term LP relationships and referral sources.

**Emotional alignment:** Physician LPs are not investing purely for financial returns. Many are motivated by the opportunity to shape healthcare innovation from the capital side after experiencing clinical practice frustrations. This alignment is genuine and should be honored in communications, not exploited. LP materials should acknowledge the dual motivation (financial returns and clinical impact) without promising specific clinical outcomes.

## Revenue Sustainability Considerations

### Fund I Constraints
- $10M fund generates approximately $200K/year in management fees (at 2%)
- This funds a lean operation: the GP team is likely not drawing full-time salaries from the fund
- Brand and marketing investments must be highly efficient
- The co-management structure with FortySix Venture Capital likely shares some operational costs

### Path to Fund II Economics
- A larger Fund II ($20-30M) would generate $400K-$600K+ in annual management fees
- Increased management fee revenue enables dedicated marketing/content staff, event production budget, and more sophisticated LP reporting
- Fund I track record is the prerequisite for Fund II fundraising
- GP carry from Fund I success is the long-term economic driver

### Ancillary Revenue Considerations
- **Advisory fees:** Some VC funds charge portfolio companies for advisory services. This is uncommon at seed stage and can create misaligned incentives. Not recommended for Fund I.
- **Event revenue:** Women's Health Nexus could theoretically generate sponsorship revenue, but monetizing the event risks compromising its positioning as a mission-driven convening. Internal cost offset through selective sponsorship may be appropriate; aggressive monetization is not.
- **Content monetization:** Substack or newsletter content could be monetized (paid subscriptions), but building audience at this stage is more valuable than revenue. Free content is recommended for the first 12-18 months.
