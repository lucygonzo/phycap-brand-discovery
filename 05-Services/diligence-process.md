# Diligence Process

## What "Investing with Clinical Precision" Means as a Process

The PhyCap tagline ("Investing with Clinical Precision℠") is not a marketing phrase. It is a description of an operational reality: when a company enters PhyCap's diligence pipeline, the product is evaluated by physicians who have practiced in the clinical domains where the product will be used. This section makes that process concrete.

---

## The Multi-Specialty Clinical Review

When a portfolio company comes in for diligence, PhyCap's GP team does not evaluate it as a single unit. Each GP reviews the deal through the specific clinical lens their specialty provides. A generalist VC asks: "Is this a good investment?" PhyCap's GPs ask: "Will this product survive contact with actual clinical practice in my specialty?"

### What Each GP Catches That a Generalist VC Would Miss

**Paul Slosar, MD, MHCDS (Orthopedics/Spine Surgery)**
- **Asks**: How does this product fit into a surgical workflow? Will a surgeon stop mid-procedure to use it? Does the adoption pathway require changing established surgical protocols?
- **Catches**: Products that look good in a demo but fail in the operating room. Technology that adds steps to already time-pressured surgical workflows. SaMD products whose regulatory strategy underestimates the clinical evidence requirements for surgical adoption.
- **Systems lens (MHCDS)**: How does this product interact with the broader delivery system? Does it create downstream workflow changes in recovery, physical therapy, or follow-up care? What happens to adjacent clinical processes when this technology is introduced?

**Robin Noble, MD, MHCDS (OB-GYN)**
- **Asks**: Does this women's health product address a genuine clinical gap, or a market gap? Is the clinical evidence plan designed for the specific physiological and care-delivery realities of OB-GYN practice? Will OB-GYNs actually adopt this, or is it designed by people who have never worked in this specialty?
- **Catches**: Women's health products built on male-normed clinical assumptions. Maternal health monitoring tools that do not account for the reality of labor and delivery workflows. "FemTech" products that repackage consumer wellness as clinical tools without the evidence base to support medical-grade claims.
- **Systems lens (MHCDS)**: What is the delivery system context for this product? Does it fit into the prenatal care pathway, the acute labor and delivery environment, or the postpartum follow-up structure? Where in the delivery system does this product create the most value, and where does it create friction?

**Vipul Kella, MD, MBA (Emergency Medicine)**
- **Asks**: Does this technology work in real time? Can it function in the chaos, urgency, and unpredictability of an emergency department? Does it require internet connectivity that an ER may not reliably have? Does it require physician attention that an ER physician cannot spare?
- **Catches**: Products that perform well in controlled clinical settings but fail under the speed and pressure of emergency medicine. Diagnostic SaMD that requires more time to use than the clinical decision it supports. Workflow tools that assume physicians have 15 minutes per patient when ER physicians often have 3.

**Tim Martens, MD, PhD (Pediatric Cardiothoracic Surgery)**
- **Asks**: Can this technology be trusted in the highest-stakes clinical environment? Does it account for pediatric physiology, which differs significantly from adult physiology? Does the product's safety profile meet the standards required for the most vulnerable patient populations?
- **Catches**: Products that are designed for adult patients but marketed as pediatric-compatible without adequate evidence. Technology whose failure mode could be catastrophic in a high-acuity surgical environment. Medical devices that have not been tested against the scale and precision requirements of pediatric anatomy.

**Fred Liss, MD (Orthopedics/Hand Surgery)**
- **Asks**: How does this product perform in the outpatient surgical environment? Does it fit into the rehabilitation and physical therapy workflows that follow hand and upper extremity surgery?
- **Catches**: Products that address inpatient workflows but do not account for the ambulatory and outpatient surgery center environments where hand surgery increasingly takes place.

**Joseph Jasser, MD, MBA (Internal Medicine)**
- **Asks**: Does this product integrate with the primary care workflow? Will an internist managing 20+ patients per day add this tool to their practice? Does it generate clinical value that justifies the cognitive load of adoption?
- **Catches**: Products that solve a problem internists do not prioritize. Technology that adds documentation burden in a specialty already overwhelmed by it. Tools that do not account for the referral patterns and care coordination responsibilities of internal medicine.

**Giovanni Lorenz, MD (Radiology/Cardiothoracic)**
- **Asks**: How does this diagnostic SaMD perform against radiologist reading accuracy? What is the false positive and false negative rate? Does the product support the radiologist's workflow, or does it create an additional review step?
- **Catches**: AI-driven imaging products whose accuracy claims are based on curated datasets rather than real-world clinical image quality. Diagnostic tools that generate results a radiologist must still verify, adding work rather than reducing it.

**Alex Tang, PA-C, MBA (Physician Assistant)**
- **Asks**: Will this product work in the hands of the entire care team, not just the attending physician? Does it account for the workflow of PAs, nurse practitioners, and other advanced practice providers who deliver a significant percentage of clinical care?
- **Catches**: Products designed exclusively for physician workflows that ignore the reality of team-based care delivery. Technology that requires physician-level credentials to operate when PAs and NPs are the primary users.

## The Diligence Process as a Brand Asset

The process described above is the operational expression of PhyCap's brand. When the website says "Investing with Clinical Precision," this is what it means:

1. **Deal enters pipeline.** 500+ deals screened annually from network referrals, inbound applications, event connections, and GP personal networks.

2. **Thesis alignment check.** Does the deal fit within the three pillars (Workflow Optimization, SaMD, Women's Health)? Explicit exclusion of life sciences and pharmaceuticals.

3. **GP specialty assignment.** The deal is routed to the GP(s) whose clinical specialty is most relevant. A surgical workflow product goes to Paul and Fred. A women's health diagnostic goes to Robin. A real-time emergency tool goes to Vipul. A diagnostic imaging AI goes to Giovanni.

4. **Clinical diligence.** The assigned GP(s) evaluate the product through their specialty lens: Will it work in their clinical environment? Does the clinical evidence plan support the claims? Is the adoption pathway realistic for their peers?

5. **Systems review.** Paul and Robin apply their MHCDS training to evaluate the product's interaction with the broader delivery system. Not just "does it work?" but "how does it change the workflow around it?"

6. **Cross-specialty check.** The broader GP team weighs in on dimensions outside the primary reviewer's specialty. An orthopedic workflow tool may have implications for internal medicine referrals, radiology imaging, or emergency department triage.

7. **Investment decision.** The GPs who reviewed the deal are the same GPs making the investment decision. No advisory board intermediary. No external consultants overriding clinical judgment. The physicians in the room are the investors.
