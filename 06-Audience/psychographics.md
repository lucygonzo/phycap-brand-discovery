# Audience Psychographics

## Physician LP Psychographic Profile

### Values

**Clinical Integrity Above All.** These physicians chose medicine because they believe in evidence-based decision-making. They carry that standard into every domain, including investing. A claim without data is a claim without credibility. When PhyCap presents its thesis, the specificity matters: "clinical care delivery workflow optimization" lands differently than "digital health innovation" because the first statement reflects a diagnostic perspective and the second sounds like a press release.

**Agency in Systems They Navigate.** The physicians drawn to PhyCap are not satisfied being excellent clinicians within a broken system. They want to reshape the system. The MHCDS and MBA credentials on the GP team are not decorative; they signal a cohort of physicians who invested years in learning how healthcare delivery operates at the organizational level. LPs are drawn to this because they share the same dissatisfaction with being excluded from structural decisions.

**Peer Quality Over Network Size.** These physicians would rather be in a room with 15 people who challenge their thinking than a Slack channel with 650 members posting introductions. The Scrub Capital model (broad clinician community, teal branding, wide net) is the contrast, not the template. PhyCap LPs value curation, credibility, and intellectual density.

**Stewardship, Not Disruption.** The language of "disrupting healthcare" alienates this audience. They do not want to disrupt the system they have spent decades building expertise within. They want to improve it, optimize it, and ensure that the next generation of clinical tools is designed with physician input. The word "stewardship" resonates. The word "disruption" does not.

### Beliefs

- The healthcare system's sustainability crisis is a capital allocation problem, not a clinical talent problem
- Physicians who understand both medicine and business are uniquely positioned to evaluate healthcare technology
- The best clinical tools are designed by people who have actually used inferior versions at 2 AM during a call shift
- Women's health has been systematically underfunded, and the market opportunity is a direct consequence of that neglect
- Venture capital is not inherently incompatible with clinical values, but most VC firms operate in ways that are

### Fears

- **Irrelevance.** That the tools shaping their daily practice will be designed, funded, and deployed without their input, and that by the time they realize it, the patterns will be set
- **Naivete.** That participating in venture capital without financial training will expose them to sophisticated operators who take advantage of their clinical focus
- **Reputation Risk.** That association with a failed fund or a poorly chosen portfolio company could damage their standing in clinical and academic circles
- **Illiquidity Regret.** That committing $150K-$500K to a 10-year fund will feel constraining if personal financial circumstances change
- **Tokenism.** That "physician-led" is a marketing label and they will be asked for capital but not for clinical judgment

### Media Consumption and Intellectual Habits

**Publications They Read:**
- *New England Journal of Medicine (NEJM)* and *JAMA* are the baseline. Not reading these would be professionally negligent. The formatting, citation standards, and tone of these publications set the bar for what "credible" looks like in written form.
- *Health Affairs* for health policy and economics. The physicians drawn to PhyCap are the ones who read Health Affairs, not just their specialty journals.
- *Harvard Business Review (HBR)* selectively. They read the healthcare and leadership sections. They do not read it cover to cover.
- *STAT News* for healthcare industry reporting. Fast, specific, non-sensational. This is how they stay current on FDA decisions, health system mergers, and digital health developments.
- *Becker's Hospital Review* for the operational side of health system management.
- *Axios Vitals* (newsletter) for daily healthcare policy and market signals in a format that respects their time.

**Publications They Do Not Read (and Why It Matters):**
- *TechCrunch* and *The Information* are not part of their media diet. Content that assumes fluency in VC terminology, startup lingo, or Silicon Valley cultural references will not land.
- *Forbes* and *Inc.* health tech coverage reads as promotional, not analytical. These physicians can distinguish between journalism and content marketing.

**Podcasts and Audio:**
- Healthcare-specific podcasts during commutes and between cases (typically 20-30 minute episodes)
- Grand rounds and CME content (they are accustomed to structured educational formats)
- Some consume general business podcasts (Patrick O'Shaughnessy's *Invest Like the Best* if it features a healthcare guest, not as a regular listen)

**Social Media:**
- LinkedIn is the primary professional social platform. They post infrequently but scroll daily. A well-constructed LinkedIn post from a peer physician carries more weight than a newsletter from a media brand.
- X/Twitter is used by some for medical Twitter (#MedTwitter) discussions, but engagement is declining. The physicians most active on medical Twitter tend to be academic or policy-oriented.
- They do not use Instagram, TikTok, or Facebook for professional purposes. Content strategies that rely on these platforms will not reach them.

**Conferences and Events:**
- Specialty society annual meetings (AAOS, ACOG, AHA, RSNA, etc.) are mandatory professional touchpoints
- Physician-executive programs (Dartmouth MHCDS, Wharton HCM, Harvard MHCM) create alumni networks that function as trusted information channels
- Invitation-only gatherings at quality venues (Congressional Country Club for Women's Health Nexus) signal peer caliber. The venue is not about luxury; it is about the message that this gathering is curated, not open-enrollment.

### Professional Identity Signals

**What Makes Them Feel Credible in a Room:**
- Board certification in their specialty, maintained through active practice (not lapsed)
- Named professorships, clinical leadership titles, or practice ownership
- Publications in peer-reviewed journals (even one or two; the act of publishing signals rigor)
- Advanced degrees beyond MD (MHCDS, MBA, MPH, PhD) when combined with active clinical practice
- Association with quality institutions and programs
- Being asked for an opinion by non-physicians who recognize their domain expertise

**What Undermines Their Sense of Credibility:**
- Being treated as a "doctor with money" rather than a physician with investment-relevant expertise
- Financial products marketed to physicians with patronizing language ("most doctors are bad with money")
- Networking events that feel like sales funnels rather than intellectual exchanges
- VC pitch decks that misspell clinical terms or misunderstand basic regulatory frameworks

## Healthcare Founder Psychographic Profile

### Values

**Clinical Evidence as Currency.** These founders do not say "move fast and break things" because in healthcare, breaking things means patient harm. They value rigorous evidence, structured clinical trials, and peer-reviewed validation. Their pitch decks cite sensitivity/specificity data, not just MAU growth.

**Regulatory Competence as Competitive Advantage.** SaMD founders who have navigated FDA Class I or Class II pathways understand that regulatory clearance is a moat, not a burden. They seek investors who share this understanding rather than investors who view FDA as an obstacle to be minimized.

**Mission Alignment Without Missionary Positioning.** These founders are building businesses, not charities. They believe that addressing clinical workflow inefficiency or women's health inequity is both a moral imperative and a market opportunity. They are uncomfortable with investors who treat "impact" as a separate category from "returns."

### Beliefs

- The best healthcare products are designed by teams that include people who have worked clinical shifts
- FDA regulatory pathway is a feature, not a bug, because it creates barriers to entry that protect market position
- Health system sales cycles are long but sticky; once a product is integrated into clinical workflow, switching costs are high
- Women's health is underfunded because the decision-makers who allocate capital have historically not understood or prioritized it
- Physician investors provide qualitatively different value than financial investors in healthcare

### Fears

- **Investor Mismatch.** Taking money from investors who will push for consumer-style growth metrics in a clinical product that requires evidence-based adoption timelines
- **Pilot Purgatory.** Getting stuck in health system pilot programs that never convert to enterprise contracts
- **Regulatory Delay.** FDA timeline extensions that create cash flow pressure before the next funding round
- **Narrative Hijacking.** Investors or advisors who reframe their clinical product as a "wellness app" or "consumer health tool" to fit a more familiar VC thesis
- **Small Fund Limitations.** That a $10M Fund I cannot provide meaningful follow-on capital, leaving them to find new investors for Series B who may not share PhyCap's clinical perspective

### Media and Information Sources

- *STAT News* and *Endpoints News* for healthcare industry intelligence
- *MedTech Dive* and *MobiHealthNews* for digital health and SaMD market coverage
- *Rock Health* annual reports for digital health funding data and trend analysis
- FDA guidance documents and pre-submission meeting summaries (regulatory fluency is table stakes)
- Healthcare VC firm blogs and thought leadership (LRVHealth, a16z Bio, Catalyst Health Ventures)
- Y Combinator's healthcare vertical content for startup operational advice
- *Nature Digital Medicine* and *npj Digital Medicine* for clinical AI and SaMD evidence standards

### Professional Identity Signals

**What Makes Them Feel Credible in a Room:**
- Clinical pilot data with named health system partners
- FDA pathway documentation (pre-submission meeting completed, 510(k) filed)
- Publications or presentations at medical society conferences about their product's clinical evidence
- Advisory board composed of practicing physicians in the relevant specialty
- Having physician investors who actively engage, not just logos on a slide

**What Undermines Their Credibility:**
- Being compared to consumer health apps when they are building regulated medical software
- Investors who ask, "Can you just skip the FDA process?"
- Pitch competitions where judges have no healthcare expertise
- Being told their market is "niche" when they are addressing a population-level clinical problem

## Cross-Segment Psychographic Patterns

| Dimension | Physician LPs | Healthcare Founders |
|---|---|---|
| Primary trust signal | Peer physician credibility | Clinical evidence + regulatory competence |
| Content format preference | NEJM/JAMA-style rigor in shorter form | Data-driven analysis with market sizing |
| Event format preference | Curated, invitation-only, quality venue | Structured pitch + deep clinical dialogue |
| Social proof that matters | "10 GPs across 13 specialties" | "Investors who evaluated through a clinical lens" |
| Language that repels | "Disruption," "moonshot," "doctor money" | "Consumer traction," "pivot to wellness," "skip FDA" |
| Time of content consumption | Between cases, commute, evening after family | Early morning, late evening, weekend strategy blocks |

## Gaps in This Section

1. **No primary research validates these psychographic profiles.** The values, beliefs, and fears are constructed from LP testimonial language, GP team composition, and healthcare professional behavior patterns. Survey or interview data from actual PhyCap LPs and portfolio founders would either confirm or revise these profiles.
2. **Generational differences unaddressed.** A 55-year-old department chief and a 38-year-old early-career attending may share values but diverge significantly on media consumption, social media engagement, and comfort with alternative investments. The current profile blends these cohorts.
3. **Specialty-specific psychographic variation unmapped.** Orthopedic surgeons and internal medicine physicians may have different professional identity signals, different income profiles, and different relationships to technology adoption. The current profile treats physicians as a monolithic group.
4. **Founder psychographics lack gender and demographic nuance.** Women's health founders (a key PhyCap thesis area) may face additional psychographic pressures related to fundraising bias, credibility questioning, and market size skepticism. This deserves dedicated analysis.
5. **No data on information consumption frequency or depth.** Knowing that physicians read NEJM is useful; knowing whether they skim abstracts or read full papers, and how often they do so, would sharpen content format decisions.
